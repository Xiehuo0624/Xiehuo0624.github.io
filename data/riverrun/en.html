Overview riverrun is a sound work about Finnegans Wake. It currently has two versions: one you can play directly on this website, and one in which the 12 tracks are played on The Induction Mixer.

What both versions share is 12 synchronised looping tracks, each one a different reading of the same word. The work asks a visitor to move among them — with a hand, or with a cursor — and so to hear some things, and necessarily not to hear others.

Motivation: the sense of missing out Finnegans Wake is a book that cannot be read in full. Joyce spent seventeen years writing it, mixing more than eighty languages, with almost every word a portmanteau of several others. Dai Congrong's Chinese translation is the limit of what that task allows — but the enormous apparatus of notes it requires makes reading difficult in a different way.

What I wanted was not to "represent" that difficulty, but to make an audience go through it bodily.

The 12 tracks exist in space all at once, while a listener's hearing and physical position are both limited: to catch one image at any given moment necessarily means losing the others. That sense of missing out — produced by physical position and physiological limit — is a spatial restaging of what it is like to read the original. The visitor is no longer a passive listener but a performer who has to move in order to hear.

Two extremes are clearly distinguishable by ear. When all 12 tracks are present at low volume at once, the dense, quiet murmur of language produces a steady sense of pressure and informational overload; when a single track is pulled up on its own, the sudden clear semantics produce the focus and security of grabbing at a straw in the noise. Meaning surfaces as you move, and dissolves as you move.

Where it comes from: why this book I read this book not for its literature but because I was studying Lacanian psychoanalysis at the time. It is an unavoidable coordinate in contemporary psychoanalytic, philosophical and aesthetic research — Deleuze and Guattari discuss it in A Thousand Plateaus, where they make a well-known judgement: Joyce's words are "multiple-rooted", and while those words shatter the linear unity of the word and even of language, they re-establish a cyclic unity of the sentence, the text and knowledge.

So for me this book is less a named reference than an entry point. What I took from it was not a way of writing but a question: meaning can be pulled apart indefinitely, but once it is pulled apart, how much can one person actually hold?

How it works: taking the text apart The original's "multi-track" quality is not my invention; it is already in the text. The first word, "riverrun", can be deconstructed into river run, reverend, and Erinnerung [German] (memory) — a river running, a priest carried downstream, and memory as water, all unfolding at once inside a single word.

I turned that polysemy directly into multiple physical tracks: the different readings of one word are assigned to different tracks, each recorded in five versions with different manners of pronunciation (alternating Chinese and English). All 12 tracks are my own voice. When a visitor mixes, they are in effect completing, on Joyce's behalf, a sentence he could not stop writing.

The work was first titled Here Comes Everybody (此即人人) — which is exactly the phrase Track C ends on.

The two versions The played version: the 12 tracks are played on The Induction Mixer. They feed the 12 antennas inside the instrument's panel and are transmitted by near-field electromagnetic induction; the player holds a pickup sensor and moves it above the panel — where your hand is, that is what you hear; move away and the sound fades. A 6-minute-13-second phone video documents one such performance. The audio is stereo, but the spatial effect of a phone recording is limited.

The digital version: on this website the work becomes an interactive spatial mixer — 12 synchronised looping tracks are laid out as numbered points on a two-dimensional panel, and the visitor wanders across it with a cursor, a single virtual microphone. Drawing near a point brings that track forward; moving away lets it sink back into the texture. On multi-touch devices every finger acts as an independent microphone. The mix is rendered through a built-in parallel effect chain into a continuously breathing sound field — reverb, echoes and a slowly swelling motion entwine, forming a blurred underlay to the work.

The digital version is not a substitute for the played version but the same thing done again where there is no hardware: handing the condition "you have to move in order to hear" back to the visitor by other means.

How it relates to The Induction Mixer Besides serving as the playing interface, riverrun's digital version also re-implements the central idea of The Induction Mixer in software. What it keeps is that instrument's design aesthetic — the place you listen from is itself the composition, sources are distributed in space, and a listener has to move in order to hear. What it gives up is the attachment to hardware.

This matters to me: the aesthetic core of a work can survive the replacement of its entire medium.

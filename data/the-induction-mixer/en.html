Overview The Induction Mixer is a 12-input, 4-output spatial mixer, and a playable instrument.

It does not pass sound through cables: the 12 audio inputs each feed one of the 12 printed-circuit-board antennas inside the panel, which transmit them by near-field electromagnetic induction; you hold a pickup made from a ferrite-rod inductor above the panel, and the pickup and preamplifier turn the field back into sound. So the panel holds 12 invisible sound sources at once, each fixed in its own position. Where you move your hand is what you hear; move away and that source fades.

The panel carries 12 input level knobs, 12 input jacks and output control. It has 12 inputs but only 4 outputs — what you hear is the result of the four pickup channels moving between those 12 sources.

Motivation Usually mixing is a stage that comes after composition — the music is written first, and then the mix is made to sound good. On this instrument there is nothing but mixing: the 12 sources are given, and the only things you can change are where you listen and how much you hear. So I wanted to try one thing: what if mixing itself were composition?

A digital audio workstation dissolves mixing into a two-dimensional screen, linear faders and parameter encoders. Balancing sound becomes a visually dominated operation in which the body barely takes part — you could call it disembodiment. I wanted to do the opposite: to turn mixing and routing back into gesture in three-dimensional space. The movement of the hand, the skin's sense of position, and the boundary of an invisible electromagnetic field are made to coincide.

The instrument therefore has two states. In its played state it is a structured electromagnetic-acoustic map for one person to improvise with, privately and intuitively. In its installed state it removes the technical threshold of a conventional musical interface, letting an audience step in bodily to decipher and rebuild an electromagnetic landscape together — loosening the line of power between composer and listener.

Where it comes from Christina Kubisch's Electrical Walks was the first thing that showed me this. She sends visitors out into the city wearing sensitive wireless headphones that make the electromagnetic fields around us audible. In her own words: "Lighting systems, wireless communications, radar, anti-theft security devices, surveillance cameras, cellphones, computers, antennae, navigation systems, ATMs, Wi-Fi routers, neon signs, public transportation networks, etc, all generate electrical fields — hidden as if under cloaks of invisibility, but with incredible presence." Her The Mixer Project goes further: "Every participant rediscovers the sound world of his own city in a new way. He becomes a composer of an individual piece which he/she even can take home."

Cathy Van Eck's Between Air and Electricity: Microphones and Loudspeakers as Musical Instruments surveys how microphones and loudspeakers have been used as instruments. That book is where I started to understand the instrument as a mediator between body and sound.

Where this work differs is that I wanted to keep their philosophy and aesthetics while making the thing closer to an instrument than to an installation. So it has a playable panel, and the way you play it is by moving your hand, not by walking through a space.

How it works First, the cascade logic. The 12 inputs use Switched Tip 3.5mm jacks: signal is cascaded down from one channel's normally-closed contact to the next (1→2→3→4 and so on). When you plug external audio into any channel, the cascade arriving from the channel above is physically broken. So if you only patch into In 1, 4, 7 and 10, the instrument reorganises itself into four independent electromagnetic-acoustic blocks — with no settings and no switches. This is also where the 12-in 4-out configuration comes from.

The antennas were not designed but bought: I ordered a batch of different types and tested them one by one — at what distance, at what angle, what each of them would pick up — and settled on the type now in use. The work was mostly in testing, not in calculating.

The whole signal path is analogue — from the inputs, the antennas and the near-field layer, through the four inductive sensors to the preamplifier, with no digital stage anywhere in between. Signal capture is physically zero-latency.

The preamplifier is based on an existing design by Ciiyte (Cai Yitong); the case was also designed by Ciiyte and fabricated by JLCPCB.

Postlude The instrument itself left no problems that needed fixing — it worked as soon as it was built. It was quickly put to use in riverrun: the 12 tracks of Finnegans Wake are played through its 12 antennas. It thus turned from a work in its own right into an instrument for making something else.

But as an instrument, its own performing possibilities are still largely unexplored: I have only used it in this one piece so far.
